## The formula

> **runtime (minutes) ≈ battery Wh × 0.85 × battery health ÷ load W × 60**

0.85 is a typical inverter efficiency. Battery health is about 1.0 for a new battery and lower with age (the calculator defaults to 0.8). Battery Wh = volts × amp-hours × number of batteries: two 12 V 9 Ah batteries are 216 Wh.

At 150 W, that's about **59 minutes on paper**. Treat it as an upper bound: real runtime falls off faster than this at high load.

The verdict is **Overloaded** above 100 % of the watt rating, **Tight** above 80 % or under 10 minutes, and **OK** otherwise. Above half load, a note asks you to check the maker's runtime chart.

## Get the inputs right

- **Load:** measure it at the UPS output: most UPSes show watts or load % on the display or through NUT (`ups.realpower`, or `ups.load` × `ups.realpower.nominal` ÷ 100). Count everything on the battery outlets; surge-only outlets don't count.
- **Watt rating:** on the label or the data sheet, next to the VA figure. A "1500 VA" unit is often 900 W.
- **Battery:** the replacement-battery spec (for example "2 × 12 V 9 Ah"), or the battery Wh on the data sheet.

## Sizing rules that hold up

1. **Size by watts, not VA.** Keep the load under about 60 % of the watt rating for runtime and headroom.
2. **Target 10 minutes, then automate the shutdown.** A UPS without automatic shutdown only delays the crash. Use NUT (or the UPS maker's software) to shut down hosts, then the NAS, in order.
3. **Pure sine wave for modern power supplies.** Active-PFC supplies can misbehave on stepped ("simulated sine") output when the UPS is on battery.
4. **Replace batteries every few years.** Runtime drops as they age. Run a self-test now and then and watch `battery.runtime`.
5. **Put the network on it too.** A server that stays up behind a dead switch is no use.
