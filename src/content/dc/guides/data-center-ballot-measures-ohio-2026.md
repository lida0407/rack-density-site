## 1. What voters decided on 3 November 2026

Data center ballot measures covered 41 votes on 3 November 2026, according to [Ballotpedia's tracker of data center-related ballot measures](https://ballotpedia.org/Data_center-related_ballot_measures,_2026). Ballotpedia counts at least 50 such local measures in 13 states this year, and Ohio accounts for 21 of them across 18 jurisdictions.

The votes on 3 November covered ten states: California, Florida, Illinois, Kansas, Maine, Nevada, New Jersey, North Dakota, Ohio and Wisconsin. By our count of Ballotpedia's table on 4 November 2026, 40 of the 41 measures had unofficial vote totals. In 39 of them the result went the way that limits development, either by approving a restriction or by rejecting a measure that would have supported a project. The exception was Harwood, North Dakota, where 76% of voters rejected a measure that would have required more review of industrial developments, data centers included. Ottawa, Kansas, showed no votes yet for its 25 MW ban.

All six measures decided earlier in 2026 also went against development, Ballotpedia says. These are unofficial results, and final counts can still change.

## 2. Ohio: bans, 25 MW caps, rezoning and tax-break guarantees

[Fortune reported on 4 November 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/11/04/ohio-data-center-ballot-measures-midterm-elections/) that at least 14 Ohio municipalities passed bans or other restrictions, and that Ohio already has 125 local data center moratoriums. Ohio has more than 200 operational data centers, Fortune says.

The Ohio measures fall into a few types. The figures below are Ballotpedia's unofficial yes and no shares.

| Type of measure | Where | Unofficial result |
|---|---|---|
| Ban above 25 MW of aggregate demand or peak load | Conneaut, Defiance, Oregon, Sunbury | Yes 61% to 79% |
| Ban on large-scale data centers | Pataskala, Trenton | Yes 74% (Pataskala), 80% (Trenton) |
| Ban on any data center in the city | Granville, Hubbard, Piqua, Upper Sandusky, Urbana | Yes 67% to 82% |
| Voter approval for projects over 50 contiguous acres, 20 MW peak demand or 500,000 gallons of water a day | Grove City | Yes 55% |
| Financial guarantees from hyperscale developers seeking special tax incentives | Perry | Yes 87% |
| Rezoning four rural residential sites to light industrial | Wilmington | No 80% on each of the four |
| Term summary for negotiations on two data centers | Ashville | No 82% |
| Advisory question on allowing data centers | Monroeville | No 88% |
| Zoning rules for unincorporated areas | Monroe and Sprigg townships | Yes 60% and 53% |

Fortune adds detail on several of these. In Sunbury, residents appear to have passed a charter amendment banning data centers that use 25 MW or more, aimed at a possible hyperscale project whose fate is unclear. The city council had already put a moratorium in place until 31 January 2027, [WOSU reported on 16 April 2026](https://www.wosu.org/politics-government/2026-04-16/sunbury-city-council-passes-moratorium-on-data-centers), for a $2 billion project at the city's 1,200-acre Business and Technology Park.

In Trenton, Fortune reports that organizers hope the ban keeps an 18-building campus out of the city, though it likely will not affect a four-building campus already under construction nearby. In Ashville, Fortune reports that residents voted to block a campus already under construction. The Monroe and Sprigg township measures were about preserving agricultural land, according to Fortune.

A statewide vote is next on the list. Ballotpedia reports that a proposed Ohio constitutional amendment banning data centers above 25 MW did not submit signatures by the 1 July 2026 deadline, and its campaign said it is aiming for the 2027 general election. Fortune cites a poll of 1,650 Ohio adults by the Ohio Northern University Institute for Civics and Public Policy with SurveyUSA, in which 57% said they would vote for that amendment and 20% against.

## 3. Outside Ohio: Illinois, Kansas, Maine and others

Ballotpedia's unofficial counts for the other states on 3 November 2026:

- Illinois: four Madison County townships (Chouteau, Granite City, Nameoki and Venice) rejected advisory questions on permitting new data centers, with 74% to 83% voting no. Joliet voters rejected an advisory question on approving AI data centers, 79% to 21%. Bloomington backed an advisory moratorium on large data centers with 78%, and Morgan backed advisory restrictions with 88%.
- Kansas: El Dorado approved a ban on high impact data centers and on Type 2 and Type 3 battery energy storage systems, 71% to 29%. Emporia approved a ban on high impact data centers, 60% to 40%.
- Maine: North Berwick approved a ban on data centers of 25,000 square feet or more. Peru and Wells approved 180-day moratoriums, and Wiscasset a six-month moratorium on applications.
- California and Florida: Alhambra (82% yes) and Edgewater (77% yes) approved bans.
- Nevada: Boulder City rejected allowing data centers in the Eldorado Valley Transfer Area, 71% to 29%.
- New Jersey: Downe Township rejected an advisory question supporting data centers, 87% to 13%.
- Wisconsin: Janesville approved a requirement for voter approval before the city can lease, sell or approve development of the GM/JATCO site for projects above $450 million.

The Madison County votes are advisory. The county had already moved on incentives: [St. Louis Public Radio reported on 26 October 2026](https://www.stlpr.org/government-politics-issues/2026-10-26/madison-county-board-bars-data-centers-receiving-tax-incentives-illinois) that the county board voted to exclude data centers from the 10-year property tax abatements of the Southwestern Madison County Enterprise Zone. The report says individual municipalities can still offer their own incentives.

## 4. What the measures restrict

For a developer, the measures sort into six kinds of limit.

1. Outright bans. Granville, Hubbard, Piqua, Upper Sandusky, Urbana, Alhambra and Edgewater prohibit data centers in the city, with no size line.
2. Power thresholds. Conneaut, Defiance, Oregon and Sunbury ban data centers above 25 MW of aggregate demand or peak load. Ottawa, Kansas, voted on a similar 25 MW line, and Mandan, North Dakota, votes on one on 15 December 2026. Grove City uses 20 MW of peak demand as a trigger for a public vote.
3. Size, land and water thresholds. North Berwick sets 25,000 square feet. Grove City adds 50 contiguous acres and 500,000 gallons of water a day as triggers.
4. Moratoriums. Peru, Wells and Wiscasset pause new applications for about six months.
5. Zoning referendums. Wilmington's four votes overturned rezonings from rural residential to light industrial. A rezoning that a council has approved can still be reversed at the ballot box.
6. Financial conditions. Perry requires financial guarantees from hyperscale developers seeking special tax incentives. Janesville and, in April, Port Washington, Wisconsin, require voter approval for large projects or tax incremental districts.

El Dorado also covers battery energy storage, which matters for sites that plan on-site storage as part of the power design.

## 5. What it means for site selection and power timelines

The most common power line in these measures is 25 MW. A cap on aggregate demand or peak load counts the whole site. As a worked example, if a town's cap applies to total facility load and the design runs at a PUE of 1.3, about 19 MW is left for IT. At 40 kW per rack that is roughly 480 racks. Check the text before you run this: some caps may count only one building, and some may add every phase on a campus together. The [rack power capacity planning guide](/resources/guides/rack-power-capacity-planning/) covers the method for turning a power limit into a rack count.

A ballot result can also arrive late in a project. Wilmington's rezonings had already been approved before the referendums reversed them, and Ashville's vote targeted a campus that Fortune describes as already under construction. A site with local zoning approval can still face a petition. A council moratorium can also hold a site while a petition moves to the ballot, as happened in Sunbury between April and November 2026.

The same pressure shows up in grid work. Our guide to [data center power line disputes](/resources/guides/data-center-power-line-disputes/) covers how transmission upgrades get held up by local opposition, and the [Australia suburban data center ban](/resources/guides/australia-suburban-data-center-ban/) shows a national government looking at location rules for the same reasons.

## 6. A checklist for developers and site selectors

1. Search the local ballot and petition record. Look for filed initiatives, referendum petitions on rezonings and moratorium resolutions in the municipality and the township.
2. Read the threshold wording. Note whether a cap uses MW, square feet, acres or water use, and whether it counts aggregate campus load, peak load or monthly demand.
3. Size the first phase against the lowest local line. If the town or a neighbour has a 25 MW cap or a 20 MW vote trigger, know what your design looks like below it.
4. Map the referendum window for each approval. Find out how long residents have to petition against a rezoning or an ordinance, and do not count the approval as final until that window closes.
5. Check grandfathering in the text. Trenton and Ashville show that construction status does not settle the question by itself.
6. Treat tax incentives as conditional. Perry's guarantees and Madison County's enterprise zone exclusion show that abatements can come with new conditions or be withdrawn.
7. List every power and storage asset. Battery storage, substations and on-site generation may fall under the same measure, as in El Dorado.
8. Publish load, water and land numbers early. Several of these measures use exactly those figures as triggers, so residents will ask for them.

## 7. Dates to watch

- 1 December 2026: Clarksville, Arkansas, votes on two referendums over ordinances authorizing up to $50 billion and $5 billion in taxable industrial development bonds for data center projects, according to Ballotpedia.
- 15 December 2026: Mandan, North Dakota, votes on a ban on data centers with an anticipated electrical capacity or demand of 25 MW or more, according to Ballotpedia.
- 31 January 2027: the end of Sunbury's council moratorium, according to WOSU.
- 2027 general election: the target of the campaign for an Ohio constitutional amendment banning data centers above 25 MW, according to Ballotpedia.
